# Why *Taiwan Matters* – Summary

## Introduction: A Small Island with Global Importance

Taiwan is often described as a small island with an outsized impact on world affairs. With a population of around 23 million, it has transformed into a flourishing democracy and a technological powerhouse – in fact, just one Taiwanese company manufactures over 90% of the advanced semiconductor chips that power the global economy ([Forever Lost in Literature: Review: Why Taiwan Matters: A Short History of a Small Island That Will Dictate Our Future by Kerry Brown](https://www.foreverlostinliterature.com/2025/01/review-why-taiwan-matters-short-history.html#:~:text=political%20and%20cultural%20entities)). But Taiwan’s significance goes far beyond economics. Its very existence stands at the heart of one of today’s most dangerous geopolitical flashpoints. China’s government considers Taiwan part of its territory and has vowed to reunify it – by force if necessary – even as Taiwan thrives as a self-governed society that sees itself as distinct. The result is a tense standoff that could, if mismanaged, spiral into a conflict drawing in the United States and other powers ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=world%E2%80%99s%20economy,thus%20a%20geopolitical%20powder%20keg)) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=the%20region,thus%20a%20geopolitical%20powder%20keg)). In *Why Taiwan Matters*, Kerry Brown explores how Taiwan’s unique history, politics, and identity have led to this fraught moment, and why the fate of this small island could **“dictate our future”** on the global stage.

Brown’s account is factual, accessible, and urgent. He takes readers on a journey through Taiwan’s turbulent history and its current predicament, breaking down complex issues into clear ideas. He shows us Taiwan not just as a pawn in a larger game, but as a place with its own people, culture, and values – a vibrant society striving to maintain its way of life in the shadow of a much larger neighbor. Through vivid examples and first-principles explanations, Brown illuminates why Taiwan’s story matters to everyone concerned about democracy, security, and the global economy. This summary will walk through the book’s key themes: Taiwan’s historical journey, the development of a distinct Taiwanese identity, its political and economic transformation, the thorny cross-strait relationship with China, and the geopolitical stakes that make Taiwan a potential flashpoint for the world.

## Historical Roots of the Taiwan-China Divide

To understand why Taiwan matters today, one must start with its history. Brown provides a concise chronicle of how Taiwan became the place it is. For centuries, Taiwan was influenced by a mix of peoples and powers from around the world. Austronesian indigenous peoples lived on the island for thousands of years. In the 17th century, European colonial powers made brief appearances – the Dutch and Spanish established outposts on Taiwan’s coasts ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Instead%2C%20Taiwanese%20people%20today%20have,%E2%80%93%20merchants%2C%20pirates%2C%20those%20fleeing)). By the late 1600s, imperial China’s Qing Dynasty extended nominal control over Taiwan, though Chinese rulers long viewed the island as a wild frontier inhabited by traders, pirates, refugees and indigenous tribes ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=landscape,home%20for%20the%20marginalized%20and)). This “porous” history meant that *“Taiwan has always been a place where influences from elsewhere wash over the society”*, as Brown notes ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Instead%2C%20Taiwanese%20people%20today%20have,%E2%80%93%20merchants%2C%20pirates%2C%20those%20fleeing)). Unlike the Chinese mainland, which developed a more unified imperial culture, Taiwan’s culture was mixed from the start – with Chinese settlers (mostly from Fujian and Guangdong provinces) living alongside indigenous groups and experiencing periods of foreign rule.

A major turning point came in 1895, when Qing China lost the First Sino-Japanese War and ceded Taiwan to Japan. For the next 50 years (1895–1945), Taiwan was a colony of Imperial Japan ([

        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=study,S)). Brown recounts how this era dramatically shaped Taiwan: the Japanese built infrastructure, industry, and a modern education system, integrating Taiwan into the Japanese empire. Memories of Japanese rule remain visible today. For example, in Taipei’s Da’an district a preserved brick wall from a former Japanese colonial prison now houses a peaceful cultural center – an “oasis of calm” repurposed from a once traumatic site ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=World%20War%2C%20the%20island%20was,amidst%20relics%20of%20the%20past)). Japanese influence can also be felt in Taiwan’s cuisine and leisure: hot spring resorts dot the mountains around Taipei, and sushi and ramen shops are as common and authentic as those in Tokyo ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Japan%20is%20present%20in%20various,feelings%20of%20attraction%20were%20reciprocated)). Brown notes that these cultural echoes of Japan, from architecture to food, illustrate how an external power left its mark on Taiwanese identity, sometimes in surprisingly positive ways. By the early 20th century, Taiwan was more developed than much of China, with a unique blend of Chinese and Japanese characteristics.

World War II brought another upheaval. In 1945, Japan’s defeat led to Taiwan being handed over to the Chinese Nationalist government (the Kuomintang, or KMT) under Chiang Kai-shek. However, mainland China was soon engulfed in the final phase of a bloody civil war between Chiang’s Nationalists and Mao Zedong’s Communists. By 1949, the Communists won control of the mainland, founding the People’s Republic of China (PRC) in Beijing. Chiang Kai-shek’s Nationalist regime fell back to Taiwan, bringing around 1–2 million refugees from China – including much of the Chinese ruling elite and military ([Forever Lost in Literature: Review: Why Taiwan Matters: A Short History of a Small Island That Will Dictate Our Future by Kerry Brown](https://www.foreverlostinliterature.com/2025/01/review-why-taiwan-matters-short-history.html#:~:text=When%20the%20bloody%20Chinese%20Civil,separate%20political%20and%20cultural%20entities)). At this moment, as Brown succinctly puts it, “two Chinas were born” ([Forever Lost in Literature: Review: Why Taiwan Matters: A Short History of a Small Island That Will Dictate Our Future by Kerry Brown](https://www.foreverlostinliterature.com/2025/01/review-why-taiwan-matters-short-history.html#:~:text=When%20the%20bloody%20Chinese%20Civil,separate%20political%20and%20cultural%20entities)): one on the mainland under Communist rule, and one on Taiwan under Chiang’s Nationalists, who continued to claim to be the legitimate government of all China (calling themselves the **Republic of China**, or ROC).

Thus, from 1949 onward, Taiwan and mainland China embarked on very different paths. Brown describes the subsequent decades in Taiwan as a *“slow march to democracy and prosperity”* ([

        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=study,S)) – but it was not straightforward. Under Chiang Kai-shek and then his son Chiang Ching-kuo, the KMT imposed authoritarian rule on Taiwan. Martial law lasted for 38 years (1949–1987), during which political opposition was harshly suppressed (a period known as the “White Terror”). Native Taiwanese (whose families had lived on the island before 1949) initially resented the mainlander-dominated KMT regime, especially after brutal incidents like the **February 28, 1947 uprising** and its suppression. However, over time, the Nationalists in Taiwan focused on economic development and education, laying the groundwork for an economic “miracle.” By the 1970s and 1980s, Taiwan industrialized rapidly – becoming one of Asia’s “Four Tigers” economies – and its people enjoyed growing prosperity ([

        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=study,S)). 

Political reform came more slowly. It wasn’t until the late 1980s that Taiwan began democratizing. Chiang Ching-kuo lifted martial law and allowed opposition parties. In 1996, Taiwan held its first direct presidential election. By the early 2000s, power peacefully transferred to a new opposition party (the Democratic Progressive Party, or DPP) after a half-century of KMT one-party rule. Brown highlights this evolution to show how Taiwan transformed from a war-torn refuge of the defeated Chinese regime into a thriving democratic nation. Today, Taiwan boasts a robust democracy with regular elections, vibrant civil society, and freedoms of speech and press – a stark contrast to the one-party authoritarian system in Beijing. This historical divergence is essential to understanding the tensions with China: each side developed its own government, society, and identity across seven decades of separation.

## Forging a Taiwanese Identity

A core theme in *Why Taiwan Matters* is the emergence of a distinct Taiwanese identity. Brown argues that **“Who are the Taiwanese?”** is a central question behind the Taiwan–China conflict ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=1)). If all Taiwanese still felt fully Chinese, reunification wouldn’t be such a contentious issue – but in reality, after decades of separate development, most people in Taiwan today see themselves as different from people in mainland China ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Who%20are%20the%20Taiwanese%3F%20What,is%20how%20Taiwan%20and%20China)). Brown delves into why and how that identity shift happened.

For much of the 20th century, identity in Taiwan was ambiguous and contested. Under Nationalist rule, the narrative was that Taiwan was simply a part of China: schools taught the history of China’s dynasties as if Taiwan were still within that “one China,” and maps in classrooms showed the Republic of China encompassing all of mainland China (and even Mongolia) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=school%20that%20their%20country%20still,again%20being%20tested%20as%20Taiwan)). Older generations, especially those who fled the mainland in 1949, often saw themselves as Chinese in exile – holding onto memories of their hometowns on the mainland and hoping for reunification one day ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Democratic%20Progressive%20Party%20,mainland%2C%20and%20were%20afflicted%20by)). Brown cites the example of Pai Hsien-yung’s 1971 novel *Taipei People*, which portrayed mainlander immigrants in Taiwan who “still pined for their old homes on the mainland” decades later ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=,with%20no%20living%20memory%20of)). For these people, being Chinese was an integral part of identity, and the notion of *Taiwanization* (emphasizing a local Taiwanese identity) felt threatening ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Democratic%20Progressive%20Party%20,two%20decades%20on%20the%20island)).

However, as the generations turned over and Taiwanese-born people with no personal connection to mainland China grew up, the sense of being uniquely “Taiwanese” strengthened. Brown provides striking statistics from modern surveys: as of 2023–24, only about **3% of Taiwan’s people identified themselves as primarily Chinese**, whereas **67% identified solely as Taiwanese**, with the remainder feeling some mix of both ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Surveys%20in%20the%20twenty,was%20carried%20out%2C%2017%20per)). Back in 1992, those numbers were very different – only 17% said “Taiwanese” and 25% said “Chinese” then ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=themselves%20as%20solely%20Taiwanese,Taiwanese%20define%20themselves%2C%20being%20solely)). Clearly, public sentiment has undergone a *“seismic and decisive”* shift ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=cent%20said%20they%20were%20Taiwanese%2C,is%20not%20one%20of%20them)). Today, *being solely Chinese is not an identity claimed by virtually anyone in Taiwan* ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=cent%20said%20they%20were%20Taiwanese%2C,is%20not%20one%20of%20them)). Instead, most Taiwanese see themselves as a distinct people, even if they acknowledge Chinese cultural roots.

Brown illustrates this identity evolution with concrete examples. One anecdote involves the humble passport: for years, Taiwan’s passport bore the country name “Republic of China,” a title appeasing Beijing by retaining the word “China.” But starting in the early 2000s, pro-Taiwan leaders pushed to highlight the name “Taiwan” instead ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=unremarkable%20document%20similar%20to%20every,that%20name%20simply%20with%20%E2%80%98Taiwan%E2%80%99)) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=tolerate%20that%2C%20even%20if%20it,that%20name%20simply%20with%20%E2%80%98Taiwan%E2%80%99)). This became a fierce debate reflecting identity – those favoring “Taiwan” wanted to assert local identity, while opponents (often older mainlander-descended Taiwanese) feared it would sever the link to the Chinese motherland ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Democratic%20Progressive%20Party%20,two%20decades%20on%20the%20island)). In 2020 a compromise was finally reached: the passport now features both “Taiwan” and “Republic of China,” with “Taiwan” displayed more prominently in English ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=did,larger%20type%20and%20more%20prominent)). As Brown explains, even this small change was symbolically significant – a tangible sign that Taiwanese people increasingly see *Taiwan* (not “China”) as their country.

Another example is language and writing. A Taiwanese friend once proudly told Brown that Taiwan still uses traditional Chinese characters in writing, unlike mainland China which adopted simplified characters decades ago ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Plenty%20of%20Taiwanese%20did%20indeed,had%20been%20China%E2%80%99s%20fate%20since)). In his friend’s view, Taiwan had preserved the richness of Chinese culture that the mainland had abandoned. Indeed, during Mao’s Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), many aspects of ancient Chinese heritage were attacked or destroyed in China, whereas Taiwan continued teaching Confucian classics and traditional ethics in schools ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=2000s%20told%20me%20proudly%20how,as%20a%20valid%20ethical%20system)). Some even said Taiwan was “**more Chinese than China**,” having saved the best of traditional culture from the tumult on the mainland ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=of%20a%20century%20earlier,was%20more%20Chinese%20than%20China)). But as Brown notes, this notion has grown more complex over time. Taiwan today doesn’t define itself only by an “ethnic Chinese” cultural lens. The society has changed with democracy and globalization, and younger Taiwanese emphasize their differences from China more than their cultural commonalities ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=In%20the%20years%20since%20that,They%20looked)) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=redeveloped,Taiwanese%20were%20saying%20they%20disagreed)).

Taiwan’s identity now is a hybrid – deeply influenced by Chinese language and heritage, yes, but also shaped by Japanese colonial history, and by global modern values. Brown highlights Taiwan as a *“place of huge variety”* in terms of culture, beliefs, and influences ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=smaller%20communities%20of%20Jews%20,and%20the%20Gods%20they%20worship)). For instance, multiple religions coexist freely in Taiwan (Buddhism, Daoism, folk religion, Christianity, etc.), making it one of the most religiously plural places in East Asia ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=fully%20accepted%2C%20even%20in%20the,5%20Unlike%20China%2C%20there%20are)) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=tight%20controls%20are%20exercised,the%20Church%20of%20Scientology%2C%20the)). This pluralism extends to social values: Taiwan was the **first country in Asia to legalize same-sex marriage** (in 2019) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=fully%20accepted%2C%20even%20in%20the,5%20Unlike%20China%2C%20there%20are)), reflecting a commitment to liberal and diverse values that sets it apart from the more conservative mainland. Civil society organizations abound – as of 2022 Taiwan had tens of thousands of social associations and hundreds of political groups ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=which%20governs%20everything,5%20Unlike%20China%2C%20there%20are)) – whereas in China, independent civic groups are heavily restricted or co-opted by the state ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=areas%2C%20Taiwanese%20and%20Chinese%20are,according%20to%20one%20report%2C%20%E2%80%98there)). All these facets contribute to an identity where Taiwanese increasingly pride themselves on being an open, democratic, and distinct society.

Crucially, Brown points out that identity is not just what one feels internally, but also how others recognize you. This is where Taiwan faces a unique predicament. Internationally, Taiwan operates like a country – it has its own government, military, currency, and issues passports that grant visa-free entry to over 140 countries ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Identity%2C%20though%2C%20is%20about%20more,when%20entering%20the%20People%E2%80%99s%20Republic)). Yet due to pressure from China, most of the world’s nations do not officially recognize Taiwan as an independent state. For example, when a Taiwanese citizen travels to mainland China, their Taiwan passport is invalid; instead, they must use a special permit and enter through lanes designated for “domestic” Chinese travelers ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Identity%2C%20though%2C%20is%20about%20more,when%20entering%20the%20People%E2%80%99s%20Republic)). Experiences like this remind Taiwanese that their status is unusual – effectively independent in practice, but not acknowledged as such by a powerful neighbor, which insists Taiwanese are Chinese citizens of an unresolved “one China.” Beijing’s official stance is that people on both sides of the Taiwan Strait *“belong to one family, one society, one state”* ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=and%20pursued%20separate%20paths,that%20are%20beyond%20one%E2%80%99s%20control)). Many Taiwanese find this frustrating, feeling that being forced to call themselves “Chinese” is like having a “shadow self” imposed on them, an identity claim beyond their control ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=and%20pursued%20separate%20paths,that%20are%20beyond%20one%E2%80%99s%20control)). Brown’s interviews and observations in Taiwan show a population that is simultaneously proud of its Chinese cultural roots and fiercely protective of its distinct political and social way of life. Today’s Taiwanese identity is the product of centuries of layered influences – Chinese, Japanese, indigenous, Western – forged by the island’s unique history. Understanding this identity is key to grasping why the people of Taiwan are not eager to be absorbed by China, and why they have charted their own course.

## From Authoritarianism to Democracy

Another major theme Brown emphasizes is Taiwan’s political transformation into a democracy and how that contrasts with China’s political system. Taiwan’s journey to democracy was gradual, but its success has made it a beacon of freedom in Asia – a fact that significantly complicates China’s goal of unification.

Under the Nationalists, Taiwan for decades was effectively a one-party state. But by the late 20th century, pressure for democratic reform grew from within society. Brown outlines how Taiwan transitioned in the 1980s and 90s: the lifting of martial law in 1987, the formation of opposition movements (like the DPP), and constitutional changes that allowed competitive elections. By 2000, Taiwan elected its first non-KMT president, ending the old single-party dominance. Today, power has alternated between parties multiple times via peaceful elections – a sign of a mature democracy. This political openness has allowed Taiwanese society to explore ideas and identities freely, further distinguishing it from mainland China, where the Communist Party brooks no dissent.

Brown draws attention to how democracy has enabled *pluralism* in Taiwan. Competing political parties represent different visions (for example, the KMT tends to favor closer ties or eventual unity with China, whereas the DPP leans toward affirming Taiwan’s separate status). Vibrant media and grassroots social movements thrive. One vivid illustration of Taiwan’s civil society was the 2014 **Sunflower Movement**, when student-led protesters occupied the legislature to oppose a trade deal with China – an impassioned, yet peaceful demonstration that would be unthinkable in mainland China. While Brown doesn’t dive deeply into that specific event, he consistently underscores that Taiwan’s citizens enjoy rights to protest, organize, and speak out that their counterparts in China do not ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=areas%2C%20Taiwanese%20and%20Chinese%20are,according%20to%20one%20report%2C%20%E2%80%98there)) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=sex%20marriages%20in%202019,they%20operate%20within%20the%20law)).

What’s particularly striking is that Taiwan’s political values now arguably align more with the West’s than with China’s. Brown notes that Taiwan has embraced principles like *democracy, human rights, and rule of law* – ideals that Sun Yat-sen (the revolutionary founder of the Republic of China) once championed, but which mainland China today does not practice ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Nationalist%20Party%20that%20he%20led%2C,principles%20and%20practises%20them%20more)) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=only%20a%20few%20weeks%20in,defender%20of%20true%20Chinese%20identity)). In an ironic twist, Taiwan preserves Sun Yat-sen’s “Three Principles of the People” (democracy, nationalism, and people’s livelihood) in its civic education, and lives by them far more than the PRC, even though the PRC claims to be the successor of Sun’s revolution ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=grandly%20at%20the%20centre%20of,defender%20of%20true%20Chinese%20identity)). This makes Taiwan a living counter-example to Beijing’s narrative that Chinese culture is incompatible with liberal democracy. It proves that ethnically and culturally Chinese people *can* build a free society – a reality that the Chinese Communist Party finds threatening to its own legitimacy.

Brown also addresses the challenges and imperfections in Taiwan’s democracy. He notes that Taiwan is not a utopia – it faces social issues common in developed nations, like low birth rates, an aging population, and even organized crime (Taiwan once had powerful mafia gangs, though crime rates today are low) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=This%20is%20not%20to%20claim,violent%20crime%20is%20not%20unknown)). There is also some xenophobia towards new immigrants; for instance, migrant workers from Southeast Asia have reported discrimination in Taiwan ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Taiwanese%20are%20deeply%20networked%20with,in%20online%20hate%20speech%20in)). Brown highlights these points to show that while Taiwan values tolerance, there are still debates about how far that tolerance extends ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=reasonable%20numbers%20to%20take%20up,how%20far%20this%20toleration%20extends)). In short, Taiwan’s democracy is vibrant but still maturing, dealing with real-world issues. Yet, despite any flaws, the fundamental political freedom in Taiwan sets it worlds apart from the authoritarian system in China.

Perhaps most poignantly, Brown describes the psychological effect on Taiwanese of living under constant threat from China. He calls it a “persistent underlying sense of crisis” that permeates life in Taiwan ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Being%20Taiwanese%20and%20the%20Challenge,of%20Normal)). After all, Taiwan’s people know that their neighbor –  just 100 miles across the strait – has missiles aimed at them and has not renounced using force to absorb them. Beijing’s stance effectively makes *Taiwanese identity* a potential trigger for war; as Brown notes, saying “I am Taiwanese” can be a far riskier statement than saying “I am American” or “I am British,” especially if a PRC nationalist is listening ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=high%20on%20the%20list,riskier%20statement%2C%20particularly%20if%20there)). Despite this cloud hanging overhead, daily life in Taiwan goes on normally. One remarkable aspect visitors notice (and which Brown emphasizes) is *“the ways in which normality prevails”* in Taiwan despite the external threats ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=One%20of%20the%20unnerving%20things,a%20conflict%20also%20placed%20into)). People go to work, school, and enjoy freedoms as anyone would in a peaceful country. This resilience – carrying on an ordinary life in the face of an extraordinary geopolitical predicament – speaks to the courage and spirit of the Taiwanese people. It also underscores why they are determined to protect their democratic way of life, since they have so much to lose if it were taken away.

## An Economic and Technological Powerhouse

Taiwan’s importance is not only political but also economic. Brown highlights Taiwan’s economic “miracle” and its current role as a critical node in the global economy. In the post-war decades, Taiwan went from a poor agrarian land to an industrial exporter renowned for high-quality manufacturing. By the late 20th century, it became a leader in electronics – known for producing everything from computers to consumer gadgets. This economic success story turned Taiwan into one of Asia’s richest societies, with living standards on par with Western nations.

Today, Taiwan is most famous for its dominance in the **semiconductor industry**. Semiconductors (the microchips that run all modern electronics) are often called the “brains” of everything from smartphones to cars. And Taiwan is the undisputed global leader in making them. Brown cites a stunning fact: *one Taiwanese company produces over 90% of the world’s advanced semiconductors* ([Forever Lost in Literature: Review: Why Taiwan Matters: A Short History of a Small Island That Will Dictate Our Future by Kerry Brown](https://www.foreverlostinliterature.com/2025/01/review-why-taiwan-matters-short-history.html#:~:text=political%20and%20cultural%20entities)). That company is TSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company), the world’s largest contract chipmaker. TSMC’s factories in cities like Hsinchu and Tainan fabricate chips for tech giants around the globe – chances are, the phone in your pocket or the computer on your desk has critical components made in Taiwan. This gives Taiwan enormous strategic leverage: the world needs its chips. It’s often said that Taiwan’s “silicon shield” is a deterrent against conflict – disrupting Taiwan would disrupt the entire global tech supply chain.

Brown explains how Taiwan came to hold this crucial position. Decades of investment in education and technology, support for high-tech industries, and integration into global trade networks all played a part. By focusing on high-value manufacturing and innovation, Taiwan stayed economically relevant even as other countries caught up in basic manufacturing. The author likely provides examples of companies and products to illustrate Taiwan’s tech prowess. Beyond semiconductors, Taiwan is home to firms like Foxconn (which assembles iPhones) and Acer (computers), and it excels in fields like biotechnology and green energy. The island’s economy is export-driven and highly competitive.

 ([File:TSMC in Tainan Science Park.JPG - Wikipedia](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:TSMC_in_Tainan_Science_Park.JPG)) *A Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) facility in Tainan Science Park, Taiwan. TSMC is the world’s leading chip manufacturer – Brown notes that a single Taiwanese company (TSMC) makes over 90% of the advanced chips used globally ([Forever Lost in Literature: Review: Why Taiwan Matters: A Short History of a Small Island That Will Dictate Our Future by Kerry Brown](https://www.foreverlostinliterature.com/2025/01/review-why-taiwan-matters-short-history.html#:~:text=political%20and%20cultural%20entities)). This technological edge gives tiny Taiwan an outsized importance in the global economy.* 

The economic ties between Taiwan and mainland China form another layer of complexity. Over the past few decades, despite political tension, trade and investment have blossomed across the strait. China is Taiwan’s largest trading partner, and many Taiwanese businesses have operations in China. This interdependence means Beijing has some economic leverage over Taiwan (for instance, at times it has restricted Chinese tourism to Taiwan to signal displeasure with Taipei’s policies ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=springs%20so%20common%20in%20Japan,spending%20tourist))). Yet it also means China’s economy would suffer if conflict broke out, since Chinese firms rely on Taiwanese tech and know-how. Brown touches on these nuances to show that economically, Taiwan and China are entangled even as politically they stand apart.

For a general audience, Brown uses relatable terms to convey Taiwan’s economic role. He might say: imagine a small island producing the chips that run iPhones, cars, and data centers worldwide – that’s Taiwan. Its importance to global supply chains is like Saudi Arabia’s importance to oil. This is a big reason “why Taiwan matters” – any instability there could wreak havoc on the worldwide economy, from stock markets to manufacturing lines. And conversely, it’s a reason the world has a stake in Taiwan’s security. It’s not just an abstract principle of democracy; it’s also about keeping our modern digital world running.

## Cross-Strait Tensions: Beijing and Taipei at Odds

At the heart of the book is the examination of the **China–Taiwan standoff**, which Brown calls one of the most dangerous flashpoints on Earth ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=An%20island%20nation%E2%80%99s%20troubled%20waters)). After laying out the history, identity, and success of Taiwan, Brown zeroes in on how these factors collide with the ambitions of the Chinese government. Why is China so fixated on Taiwan? And how does Taiwan cope with living next to a giant neighbor that claims it?

Brown explains that the Chinese Communist Party views Taiwan through the lens of historical nationalism. To Beijing, Taiwan is the unfinished business of China’s recovery from the “Century of Humiliation.” After all, Taiwan was taken by Japan in 1895, and when Mao’s communists won in 1949, they inherited a China missing this piece (since Chiang Kai-shek held onto it). The PRC insists that there is only “one China” and Taiwan is part of it – a renegade province that must eventually be reunified. This position is enshrined in China’s rhetoric and even its laws (for example, China’s 2005 Anti-Secession Law explicitly authorizes force if Taiwan formally declares independence). For the Chinese leadership, reunifying Taiwan would cement their legitimacy and national pride – particularly under President Xi Jinping, who has stoked nationalist sentiment and stated that the Taiwan issue cannot be passed down indefinitely. Brown describes how under Xi, China has grown more assertive and **“bellicose”** regarding Taiwan ([Forever Lost in Literature: Review: Why Taiwan Matters: A Short History of a Small Island That Will Dictate Our Future by Kerry Brown](https://www.foreverlostinliterature.com/2025/01/review-why-taiwan-matters-short-history.html#:~:text=Taiwan%20is%20now%20a%20flourishing,Taiwan%20is%20thus)), ramping up military pressure.

The military pressure is real and growing. Brown provides concrete figures that illustrate this trend: In 2020, Chinese warplanes violated Taiwan’s Air Defense Identification Zone some **381 times**; by 2022, that number had skyrocketed to **1,727 incursions** ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=Of%20all%20the%20flashpoints%20around,to%20Taiwan%2C%20while%20a%20new)). This dramatic increase in Chinese military drills near Taiwan shows Beijing’s willingness to intimidate. China regularly sails warships and flies fighter jets around the island as a warning. The People’s Liberation Army has also been rapidly modernizing, building capabilities (like amphibious invasion forces and ballistic missiles) aimed at one day being able to attack Taiwan. From Taiwan’s perspective, this is an existential threat. The Taiwanese military must stay on constant alert, and the society practices civil defense drills. Brown likely references incidents such as China’s live-fire exercises in 1995–96 (when it fired missiles into waters near Taiwan to protest a Taiwanese election) and more recent exercises like those in 2022 after a high-profile U.S. visit to Taipei. Each episode reminds the world how easily sparks could fly.

Yet, Taiwan has not capitulated to this pressure. As Brown notes, a **“new generation of Taiwanese people”** is more pro-independence than ever ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=forces%20in%201949%2C%20and%20the,for%20Taiwan%20that%20will%20deter)). Many young Taiwanese grew up after martial law, enjoying democracy and distinct civic life; they feel little attachment to China and instead want normal country status for Taiwan. Politically, outright declaring independence is tricky – Taiwan’s leaders understand that a formal declaration of independence could trigger a Chinese invasion. So the current strategy in Taipei is maintaining an uneasy **status quo**: Taiwan exists as a self-governing entity (with its own democratic government and military), but it stops short of legally declaring itself a separate nation from China. This ambiguous status quo has held since the late 1970s, and Brown suggests it might be the only viable path for now to avoid war ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=China%20while%20not%20provoking%20Beijing,%E2%80%9D)). In the book’s conclusion, he notes that any attempt to abruptly change this situation – either Beijing trying to force unification, or Taiwan declaring full independence – could be catastrophic. He even quotes his own stark assessment: defending the current stalemate is the only sensible option, and *“anything else is insanity”* ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=China%20while%20not%20provoking%20Beijing,%E2%80%9D)).

Brown examines how both sides navigate this delicate impasse. For China, he explains, the calculation is difficult. Some in Beijing might believe they could successfully invade Taiwan, but Brown is not convinced it would be easy ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=against%20a%20communist%20superpower,with%20Taiwan%20remaining%20ambiguously%20half)). Taiwan’s terrain is defensive (it’s an island with rough seas and few suitable beaches for landing), and its military, while smaller than China’s, is highly motivated to defend home soil. Moreover, an invasion could trigger intervention by the United States and possibly Japan and other U.S. allies – leading to a wider war that China would want to avoid ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=the%20region,thus%20a%20geopolitical%20powder%20keg)) ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=blockade%20the%20island%20and%20mount,thus%20a%20geopolitical%20powder%20keg)). So China currently pursues a strategy of grey-zone coercion: diplomatic isolation of Taiwan, economic incentives and punishments, and military posturing short of open war. Meanwhile, Taiwan’s strategy under leaders like current President Tsai Ing-wen has been “soft resilience.” Taiwan strengthens its defenses and international partnerships quietly, but avoids provoking China unnecessarily. It refuses Beijing’s calls for political talks under the precondition of “one China,” yet it also refrains from any move that could be seen as a formal split. This precarious dance is ongoing and requires constant calibration.

## The U.S. and the International Balancing Act

Taiwan’s predicament doesn’t play out in a vacuum – it’s deeply tied to the broader geopolitical tug-of-war between the United States (and its allies) and China. Brown underscores that for the U.S. and many Western countries, Taiwan is a critical partner and a symbol of democratic values in Asia. At the same time, these nations must be careful in how they support Taiwan, to deter Chinese aggression without triggering it. It is a classic tightrope walk of international diplomacy.

The United States has a particularly central role. After 1949, the U.S. initially backed Chiang Kai-shek’s Taiwan as the “free China” against Mao’s communist regime. During the Cold War, Taiwan was a formal U.S. ally and even held China’s seat at the United Nations until 1971. In 1979, the U.S. switched diplomatic recognition to Beijing (acknowledging the PRC as China’s government), but it did not abandon Taiwan. Instead, Washington adopted a policy of **“strategic ambiguity”** – it doesn’t officially recognize Taiwan as independent, but it also makes sure Taiwan can defend itself. The U.S. Congress passed the **Taiwan Relations Act (1979)**, which commits the U.S. to assist Taiwan in maintaining its self-defense capabilities and declares any attempt to coerce Taiwan a matter of “grave concern.” However, the act stops short of an explicit defense guarantee. This ambiguity is deliberate: it aims to caution Beijing against using force, yet also discourage Taiwan from unilaterally declaring independence (which could drag the U.S. into war). Brown describes this as a *“delicate balancing act for the West”* – providing enough support to **deter** China *“while not provoking Beijing.”* ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=generation%20of%20Taiwanese%20people%20want,government%20may%20believe%20that%20it)).

In practical terms, the U.S. supports Taiwan through arms sales (such as fighter jets, missiles, and naval ships) and by maintaining a strong military presence in the Pacific. U.S. Navy vessels regularly patrol the Taiwan Strait to assert it as international waters. American military advisors quietly help train Taiwanese forces. Politically, though the U.S. doesn’t have an embassy in Taipei (it has an unofficial institute), high-level visits and statements of support have increased in recent years, especially as China’s rhetoric heats up. Other countries like Japan, Australia, and many European nations have also voiced growing concern about Taiwan’s security. Brown likely notes that Taiwan’s fate is now often discussed alongside other global issues of democracy and authoritarianism, similar to how Russia’s actions in Ukraine alarm the world. Taiwan is seen as a bellwether: if a large authoritarian power can swallow a smaller democratic neighbor, what does that mean for the international order?

The U.S. and other democracies view Taiwan as a “bastion of freedom” in Asia ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=its%20companies%20produces%20over%2090,thus%20a%20geopolitical%20powder%20keg)), making its defense strategically and ideologically important. At the same time, this vibrant normalcy persists under the shadow of Chinese military threats, creating a constant balancing act in the region.* 

Brown paints the scenario of what could happen if tensions boil over. Should China move to invade or blockade Taiwan, the U.S. would face a fateful choice: intervene militarily or watch a democratic friend fall. The risk of superpower conflict would be extremely high – some analysts even call Taiwan the trigger point for a potential World War III. Because of this, Brown and many experts argue that maintaining peace across the Taiwan Strait is of paramount importance to global stability. Even short of war, any severe crisis over Taiwan (like a blockade or exchange of fire) could disrupt trade routes in East Asia and devastate the global economy. It’s not only chips that are at stake; a conflict would likely halt the flow of countless goods shipped through the region and could spook markets worldwide.

One key message Brown delivers is that *everyone has an interest in avoiding conflict over Taiwan*. He suggests that for now, the status quo – Taiwan retaining its de facto independence without a formal declaration, and China holding off on use of force – however unsatisfactory or awkward it may be, is preferable to the alternative of war ([

        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=China%20while%20not%20provoking%20Beijing,%E2%80%9D)). Thus, the international community should strive to **“strenuously defend”** this peaceful stalemate ([

This might involve deterring aggression (through military preparedness and clear signaling that an attack would have consequences), while also engaging diplomatically to prevent misunderstandings. It’s a delicate equilibrium, and Brown acknowledges it’s an uneasy one – but until or unless China’s political system changes or a mutually agreeable solution is found, it’s the most realistic path to keep Taiwan safe and the peace intact.

## Conclusion: Taiwan’s Future and Why It Matters

In wrapping up *Why Taiwan Matters*, Brown brings together the threads of history, identity, politics, and geopolitics to underscore a pivotal point: **Taiwan’s fate will have ripple effects far beyond its shores.** This small island encapsulates many of the key struggles of our time – democracy versus authoritarianism, the rights of people for self-determination versus the ambitions of great powers, and the interdependence of the global economy. As one reviewer noted, Brown’s book is a *“cogent and unsettling exploration”* of this critical issue ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=An%20island%20nation%E2%80%99s%20troubled%20waters)). It leaves readers with a clear understanding that Taiwan is not a remote, niche concern, but a central challenge that could shape the 21st century.

Brown emphasizes respect for the people of Taiwan and their wishes. He portrays them not as pawns, but as 23 million individuals who have built a thriving society and just want to live in peace and freedom ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=Why%20Taiwan%20Matters%20helps%20us,accessible%2C%20expert%20and%20urgent%20way)). He also acknowledges the Chinese perspective – the powerful pull of nationalism and historical sentiment that makes Taiwan so important to Beijing. Yet, he suggests that coercion or war would be a tragedy for all involved. The hope lies in careful statecraft, international solidarity in support of peaceful resolution, and perhaps long-term changes (for instance, if China were to evolve politically or if cross-strait dialogue could resume on respectful terms). Until then, Taiwan will continue to walk a tightrope, bolstered by its resilience and the support of like-minded nations.

In the end, why does Taiwan matter? Brown’s answer is multi-faceted. Taiwan matters because it is a living proof that democracy can flourish in Chinese-speaking soil, providing an alternative model that challenges authoritarian narratives. It matters because it’s a linchpin of the global economy’s high-tech future – the chips in our devices and the innovation driving progress flow through Taiwan. It matters because of its strategic location in the Asia-Pacific, astride vital sea lanes – control over Taiwan could shift regional power balances. And most of all, Taiwan matters because real people’s lives and liberties are at stake in how this conflict is resolved. In Brown’s view, standing with Taiwan is not just about supporting a friend, but about defending principles of freedom and preventing a potentially catastrophic war.

The book leaves readers with a nuanced appreciation for Taiwan’s unique story. Brown has taken us from the mists of its early history to the frontlines of its modern-day showdown, all in an engaging narrative accessible to non-experts. We come away understanding that Taiwan’s past has shaped its present identity – an identity that won’t simply consent to being erased. We also see clearly how a clash over Taiwan could affect us all, whether through economic shock or the unthinkable prospect of superpower war. Taiwan may be small in size (roughly the area of Maryland, as Brown quips ([
            WHY TAIWAN MATTERS | Kirkus Reviews
        ](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/kerry-brown/why-taiwan-matters/#:~:text=has%20become%20increasingly%20assertive%20about,for%20Taiwan%20that%20will%20deter))), but it looms large in the questions of our era. As one commentator in the book notes, **“if you want to understand the twenty-first century, you need to understand Taiwan.”** ([Why Taiwan Matters](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250362094/whytaiwanmatters/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CIf%20you%20want%20to%20understand,Act%3A%20America%E2%80%99s%20End%20in%20Afghanistan))

Brown’s final message is one of cautious optimism mixed with realism. Taiwan’s story is one of resilience – time and again it has defied odds, whether surviving colonial domination, authoritarian rule, or diplomatic isolation. That resilience gives hope that a peaceful future is possible. However, prudence is required: all sides must handle the situation with care. In Brown’s words, defending the current peace is paramount; any drastic move otherwise could be “insanity.” In sum, *Why Taiwan Matters* teaches us that Taiwan’s challenges are the world’s challenges. By learning Taiwan’s story, we gain insight into broader themes of history and destiny that will shape our collective future. Keeping Taiwan free, prosperous, and secure is more than a local issue – it is a bellwether for the values and stability of the world we will all share in the years ahead.
